# Quality standard: how a good explainer feels

Read when picking the look and reviewing segment 1 (Step 04), when a builder reviews its own
segment (Step 05), and for the polish and second pass (Step 07). Step 03 reads sections 1 and 2.

Whether a film looks good, explains clearly and keeps people watching is a feeling. It is judged
by watching, never scored, and code only catches hard faults. This page lists twelve things a
viewer with taste notices. It is guidance to apply with judgment, not a checklist to pass, and it
prescribes no style: every look has to meet it.

The one ruler: **would a viewer finish this believing careful work went into it, want to keep
watching, and understand it?**

## How to watch

- **Straight through, like a viewer.** Once, without stopping. Note where you were hooked,
  drifted, did not understand, were pulled out, or felt it was cheap. Give a video-capable model
  the whole file; otherwise use frames at one-second spacing. Video review is slow, so use it for
  segment 1, the first cut and the final cut; use stills the rest of the time.
- **Then look closely.** Contact sheet and full-size frames of the key moments: where did each
  feeling come from?
- **Side by side with something better**: your reference moments, a film you admire, or your own
  previous version. "Is this moment in the same league?"
- **By someone who did not build it.** Segment builders are the wrong judges of their own work.
- **Conclude in words**: "good enough", or "fix these things, because...". If you cannot say what
  is good about it, it is not good enough yet. No scores.

## The twelve things

Each: what it feels like when it works, the signs it feels cheap, and a question to ask.

**1. Promise and hook.** It starts with the subject, and the first picture keeps the cover's
promise. Cheap: greetings, background, an intro sting, asking for a subscription first. Ask: if I
had scrolled onto this, would I swipe away in the first fifteen seconds? The hook lives mostly in
the narration; if it is weak, tell the user.

**2. Structure and retention.** Each part moves forward and makes you want the next; it stops when
it is done. Cheap: a flat middle, the same point made several ways, parts joined like a list, an
ending that only repeats. Ask: where did I start drifting, and was anything new there?

**3. Clarity.** The picture arrives as the word is said and the eye knows where to look. Cheap:
screens full of text, the narration retyped, a complex diagram shown all at once. Ask: with the
sound off, can I see the structure? Is anything on screen not serving this sentence?

**4. Metaphor and evidence.** Abstract ideas get concrete pictures that stick; when the voice says
"it works", the picture shows it working. Cheap: icons and text cards instead of explanation, a new
metaphor every part, a film about a tool that rarely shows the tool. For tools and workflows the
real interface is the main evidence: whole screen first, then push in to the readable part.

**5. Shots and change.** Every shot has a job; consecutive shots differ in size, subject or
direction. Cheap: one shot held while elements swap inside it, a camera that drifts for no reason,
the same push-in every time. Ask: if this shot were cut, would the film lose anything?

**6. Composition and background.** Each moment has one clear lead; the frame breathes; settled
things are square. Cheap: two things fighting for the lead, small things huddled in the middle,
stacks of cards, an empty area that looks forgotten. Ask: freeze a frame; where does the eye land,
and is that the right place?

**7. Presenter and frame.** The presenter sits in a designed space that shares the film's light and
color; large, corner and hidden states change for a reason. Cheap: keying fringes, a random
background, popping in and out, two heads ghosting at a jump cut, the frame covering what we need.

**8. Type and on-screen text.** Readable on a phone, few faces, text stays long enough to read.
Cheap: fine on a monitor but unreadable small, text cut off or on busy backgrounds. Text carries
keywords, numbers and conclusions, not the narration. Pictures with no words also lose people: a
few key words, hung on the spoken word.

**9. Color, light and unity.** One hand made it: restrained color, an accent that means "this
matters", believable light with a consistent direction. Cheap: each segment with its own colors,
accent everywhere, the default AI template. A whole-film question for the polish; builders do not
hunt for it.

**10. Motion, transitions and rhythm.** Movement has weight, starts and stops naturally, and helps
understanding. Cheap: constant-speed slides, everything fading in together, things appearing or
vanishing in one frame, busyness for its own sake. Rhythm alternates: time where information is
dense, speed where emotion builds, a breath between chapters.

**11. Sound.** The voice is clear and close; music steps back when someone speaks; effects match
their material. Cheap: music covering the voice, too many or too loud effects, the wrong material,
room tone vanishing. Loudness and true peak are specs, measured in Step 06.

**12. Credibility and polish.** Nothing pulls you out. Cheap: something that should be tucked in
shows, corners stick out at joins, a hand-faked interface, typos in captions. Ask: during playback,
was there any instant that made me think "this was made"?
